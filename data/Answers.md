### Q5: If the mutation is a nucleotide substitution, is it synonymous, missense or nonsense? 

The mutation is a nucleotide substitution (c.221T>C) . Since it changes the encoded amino acid (p.Ile74Thr), it is a missense mutation (not synonymous, since the amino acid changes, and not nonsense, since no stop codon is created). 

### Q6: If nucleotides have been inserted or deleted, could the change alter the reading frame (i.e. frameshift)? 

A frameshift occurs when the number of inserted or deleted nucleotides is not multiple of three. In our case there are no insertions or deletions (no gaps in the alignment), so the reading frame is not altered and there is no frameshift. 

### Q7: How do the properties of the WT and mutant amino acids differ?  

The wild-type amino acid is isoleucine (I), which is nonpolar and hydrophobic. The mutant amino acid is threonine (T), which is polar and hydrophilic because it contains a hydroxyl group (-OH). 

Therefore, the substitution changes the position from a hydrophobic amino acid to a polar one. 

### Q8: Could the substitution change the interactions made by this position? Why? 

Yes. Isoleucine mainly participates in hydrophobic interactions, whereas threonine can form hydrogen bonds through its hydroxyl group. Therefore, the substitution could change the interactions with neighboring amino acids or with the surrounding environment. 

### Q9: Could this affect how the protein folds or functions? Why?  

Yes, it could. Replacing a hydrophobic amino acid with a polar one may affect protein folding and stability. However, the actual effect depends on the position's environment and role in the protein. 

### Q10: How does the sequence variant observed at the CDS level compare with the sequence variant observed at the protein level? 

At the CDS level, the mutant differs from the wild type by a single nucleotide substitution, c.221T>C (a T is replaced by a C at position 221). At the protein level, this appears as a single amino acid substitution, p.Ile74Thr, where isoleucine is replaced by threonine. Both levels show one change only: the sequence length is unchanged, and there are no insertions, deletions or frameshifts. The difference is that the CDS variant is a change of nucleotide (T → C), whereas the protein variant is a change of amino acid (Ile → Thr), and the variant position differs between the two levels (nucleotide 221 vs. residue 74). 

### Q11: How does the nucleotide variant in the CDS mutant sequence produce the observed variant in the amino-acid mutant sequence? 

The nucleotide at position 221 is the second base of codon 74 (nucleotides 220-222). In the wild type this codon is ATA, which encodes isoleucine (Ile). The T→C substitution changes it to ACA, which according to the genetic code encodes threonine (Thr). Since the substitution alters the codon so that it specifies a different amino acid, the result is a missense mutation, and the protein carries Thr instead of Ile at that position. The rest of the codons are unchanged, so the remaining protein sequence is identical to the wild type.

### Q13: Are there any sequence positions with variants across the three species, either substitutions or gaps in the CDS sequences?

Yes, there are many substitutions. Between human and dog there are 72 nucleotide differences along the 447 nt CDS (83.9% identity), affecting 56 different codons. About 46% of these differences are at the third codon position, 39% at the first and only 15% at the second. No real insertions or deletions are expected, since the three proteins have the same length (148 amino acids) and the protein alignment shows no gaps. The few gaps that appear in the BLASTn output are most likely artefacts of the local alignment and do not represent true indels.

### Q14: Are there any highly conserved regions across the three species in the protein sequences?

Yes. The motifs FERCELARTLK (residues 21-31) and DYGIFQINSRYWCNDGKTP (residues 71-89) are identical in human, dog and giant panda. Another conserved block is AKRVV (residues 110-114), and all eight cysteines (positions 24, 48, 83, 95, 99, 113, 134 and 146) are also conserved. In total, 115 of 148 positions (77.7%) are identical in the three species.

### Q15: Are there any sequence positions with variants across the three species, either substitutions or gaps in the protein sequences?

Yes. 33 of the 148 positions are variable and there are no gaps in the alignment. Human and dog differ at 29 positions (80.4% identity), human and giant panda at 29 positions (80.4%), and dog and giant panda at only 10 positions (93.2%). For example, immediately after the conserved GTP motif (position 90), human lysozyme has glycine (G), whereas dog and giant panda have arginine (R). Other variable positions are found at the N-terminus (3, 5, 6, 10, 14 and 20) and in the regions 32-41, 96-109 and 132-145.

### Q16: Are there regions more conserved than others?

Yes. The most variable regions are the N-terminus, which includes the signal peptide (residues 3-20), the region 32-41, the region 96-109 and the C-terminal region 132-145. The most conserved regions are residues 21-31 and 71-89, together with the cysteine residues. The same pattern appears at the CDS level, where the conserved nucleotide stretches correspond to the conserved protein motifs.

### Q17: Are all nucleotide sequence variants accompanied by a corresponding, equivalent amino-acid sequence variant?

No. Between human and dog, 56 codons differ at the nucleotide level but only 29 differ at the amino-acid level, so 27 codons carry only synonymous changes. For example, codon 7 (CTG → CTT) and codon 88 (ACC → ACG) change the nucleotide sequence but still encode leucine and threonine, respectively. This is possible because the genetic code is degenerate: several codons encode the same amino acid, and most synonymous changes occur at the third codon position.
